# Stage 3 — Competitor and Adjacent-Workflow Research

**Status:** Complete (public desk research; no accounts, trials, or primary interviews)  
**Completed:** 2026-09-07  
**Scope:** Existing workflow coverage for creative agencies repeatedly producing short-form branded video and social campaign variants. This is not a feature leaderboard, market-size study, product selection, or implementation plan.

## Method, evidence rules, and selection boundary

- Every linked source was accessed on 2026-09-07. Where a source exposes a publication or update date, that date is recorded in the evidence table. Prices, plan gates, and model catalogues are volatile snapshots, not commitments.
- A vendor document is strong evidence of the vendor's stated product and commercial terms, not evidence of adoption, output quality, ROI, legal sufficiency, or a workflow problem's prevalence. User reviews and professional testing are retained only as bounded workflow signals.
- **Fact** is directly supported by a named source; **inference** connects sources; **hypothesis** is a testable proposition; **assumption** is a working condition not verified here; **unknown** means this research did not substantiate it. A blank/partial matrix cell is an unknown about public evidence, never proof a product lacks the capability.
- Coverage means a capability was documented in public material, not that it is complete, usable for every client, or connected to the other cells in the same row. An em dash means **not substantiated in the sources inspected**, not that the capability is absent.
- This stage adds a product only when it exposes a distinct agency-workflow layer. It therefore examines Runway, LTX Studio, Adobe Firefly/Creative Cloud, Kive, Frame.io, Ziflow, Canva, and Sprout Social. Magnific, ImagineArt, OpenArt, Midjourney, Krea, and ComfyUI were not separately teardowned: within this scope they would mostly repeat the stand-alone generation layer already represented by Runway/LTX. Figma AI was also out of scope because its strongest documented workflow is design-file collaboration rather than recurring short-form video production. Revisit only if interviews identify one as the actual system of record.

### Coverage key

- **● Primary** — stated as a core workflow layer.
- **◐ Partial** — stated support, but limited, adjacent, or not shown to span the full agency workflow.
- **G Governance-adjacent** — records access, provenance, checklist, or approval evidence; it is not proof of rights clearance or consent.
- **—** — no relevant public support was substantiated in this review.

**Landscape conclusion — inference, medium confidence.** The relevant market is a chain of specialized systems, not a blank canvas: generation and context (Runway, LTX, Firefly, Kive), visual proofing (Frame.io, Ziflow), and social activation (Canva, Sprout) each have credible documented coverage. That is evidence against assuming agencies need another general-purpose creative suite. It does not show whether their handoffs work together in a real agency, whether current tools are affordable, or whether a missing connection has enough economic weight to buy. [C1–C25]

## 5. Important overlaps and operating gaps

### What is already covered

1. **Reference-to-generation is increasingly a shared workspace, not just a prompt box.** Runway projects, LTX starting modes, Firefly Boards/linked documents, Kive boards, and Canva Brand Kits all provide documented ways to reuse visual context. **Fact synthesis — high confidence for availability.** [C1–C4], [C6–C7], [C10–C11], [C20–C21]

2. **Version-specific visual review is an established specialist category.** Frame.io and Ziflow explicitly associate comments/decisions with assets or proof versions and support comparisons. **Fact synthesis — high confidence for stated capability.** [C12–C18]

3. **Authorized social release is already handled downstream.** Canva and Sprout both document planning/scheduling/publishing; Sprout adds multi-step message approvals and external approvers. **Fact synthesis — high confidence for stated capability.** [C21], [C23–C25]

4. **Existing products make replacement difficult by offering integration or suite gravity.** Runway connects Agent to Figma/Dropbox/Notion; Adobe connects assets across its suite; Ziflow exposes integrations/API; Sprout imports from several DAM/creative surfaces. **Fact synthesis — high confidence for availability.** [C3], [C7], [C19], [C25]

### Gaps that public evidence still leaves open

1. **The reference, generated asset, review proof, and published post are not shown as one continuity record.** The products document adjacent stages, but the inspected evidence does not establish an end-to-end record that carries the input brief/reference, prompt/model/seed, selected output, decision, rights evidence, and channel-specific delivered variant together. **Inference — medium confidence.** This is a public-evidence gap, not a claim that no integration or custom process can do it. [C1–C25]

2. **Governance primitives solve different problems.** Firefly Content Credentials establish creation/edit metadata; Frame.io watermarks associate access with a viewer; Ziflow records decisions/checklists/audit history; LTX exposes a commercial-use term. None of those source types alone proves a campaign has verified talent consent, music/stock rights, claim substantiation, approved usage territory, or platform disclosure. **Inference — high confidence about the distinction; low/medium confidence that a given agency lacks such a system.** [C4], [C8–C9], [C15], [C17–C18]

3. **Formal proofing can be too much for a fast social post while light social approval can be too little for a video cut.** Ziflow's staged, auditable proofing and Sprout's outgoing-message approvals operate at different units of work. The appropriate minimum control is a project/risk question, not a universal feature request. **Inference — medium confidence.** [C16–C18], [C24–C25]

4. **AI collaboration does not demonstrate client decision ownership.** Runway and LTX document shared internal work; Frame.io/Ziflow/Sprout document reviewer or approver controls. None of the sources establishes that tools overcome an absent client owner, unclear scope, late legal input, or a missed decision deadline. **Inference — medium confidence.** This preserves Stage 2's process-versus-software disconfirmation test. [C1–C4], [C12–C19], [C24]

5. **Metered generation makes iteration cost a workflow constraint.** Credits are explicit in Runway, LTX, and Kive plans, while the independent LTX review observed that repeated amendments consume opaque computing allowance. The actual burden for an agency is still unknown. **Inference — medium confidence.** [C2–C5], [C11]

6. **Some apparent gaps may be integration, configuration, or process work rather than a missing product category.** Ziflow's routing/API, Adobe's ecosystem, Runway connectors, and Sprout imports all make a new standalone system less compelling unless agencies show persistent loss of context or governance despite using them. **Inference — medium/high confidence.** [C3], [C7], [C19], [C25]

## 6. What Stage 3 changes—and does not change—from Stage 2

### Changes

- **Stage 2's fragmented-tool observation is refined, not amplified.** Specialist products already cover much of the visible friction. The question is no longer “is there a review, asset, or scheduler tool?” It is whether one or more specific handoffs fail often enough that agencies cannot solve them with their existing stack and process. **Inference — medium/high confidence.**
- **Approval/version is not an unoccupied opportunity by itself.** Frame.io and Ziflow give clear incumbent alternatives, including free external reviewers and workflow/audit options. Any later opportunity must explain why the existing category does not suffice for a concrete campaign type. **Fact plus inference — high confidence for availability; medium for insufficiency.** [C12–C19]
- **“Commercially usable AI” needs a more precise test.** Provenance, access controls, approval audit, and commercial-use terms are different controls. Future research should ask which clearance evidence—if any—an agency actually needs per asset/variant and where it now lives. **Inference — high confidence.** [C4], [C8–C9], [C15], [C17–C18]
- **The highest-value comparison is against the incumbent stack, not against a blank workflow.** Existing Adobe/Canva suites and integrations create adoption resistance; any claimed time saving must include migration, budget owner, reviewer access, and channel handoff. **Inference — medium confidence.** [C3], [C7], [C19–C21], [C25]

### Does not change

- Creative agencies producing recurring branded short-form video/social variants remain the **candidate segment**, not a chosen customer or product. Stage 3 found workflow relevance, not representative demand, purchase intent, retention, or market size.
- Stage 2's core evidence still stands: review/approval, version context, control/continuity, commercial governance, and fragmented workarounds can be consequential. The competitor map shows existing ways to address parts of them; it does not establish that they work cheaply or well in the target segment.
- No final product, feature set, implementation, deployment, or roadmap follows from this stage.

## 7. Open questions and disconfirmation tests

| Open question | Minimum evidence to collect in Stage 4 / interviews | What would weaken or disconfirm it |
|---|---|---|
| Is a particular creative-to-review handoff actually broken? | Walk through one recently shipped and one delayed campaign: brief, references, prompts/models, outputs, proof links, decisions, and final posts; record the tool used at each handoff. | The agency can show its current stack preserved context and kept approvals on time with little coordinator work. |
| Are Frame.io/Ziflow already sufficient for high-stakes video review? | Ask editors/producers to compare their required routing, reviewers, version count, audit need, and annual spend with a concrete incumbent configuration. | They routinely use one of these tools successfully and the remaining issue is client responsiveness/scope, not software. |
| Is generation context needed at approval time? | Ask reviewers what they need to decide: prompt/reference/model/seed, alternative outputs, source/rights, brand checklist, or only the rendered cut; inspect a real rejected asset. | Reviewers decide reliably from the current proof/brief and do not need upstream generation context. |
| Do rights, consent, and brand-safety records change a tool choice? | Collect the actual client policy, contract clause, talent release, claims checklist, and disclosure decision for at least three externally published AI-assisted assets. | Agencies rely on vendor terms plus existing legal/DAM process and no one needs a new campaign-level record. |
| Does agency size/risk determine the right approval layer? | Compare a fast social post, paid ad variant, actor-led video, and regulated/claims-sensitive asset in the same or comparable agencies. Note reviewer count, deadline, and audit requirement. | One lightweight workflow handles all cases without material risk, or work volume is too low to justify specialized controls. |
| Are credit models a material barrier to recurring variants? | Capture planned versus actual credits/renders, rejected outputs, human edit time, and cost per approved variant for a real sprint. | Teams have predictable high acceptance and generation cost is negligible beside production labor/media spend. |
| Is a downstream social tool already the source of truth? | Ask account/social leads where approval state, channel variants, publishing authority, and performance feedback are actually maintained. | Sprout/Canva/calendar already owns the relevant decision state and upstream creative context is unnecessary. |
| Would an integration/process change solve it more cheaply than new software? | Map existing connectors, API availability, named owner, and a manual workaround cost before proposing any new surface. | A one-time integration, template, naming rule, or client approval SLA resolves the observed failure. |

## 8. Implications for Stage 4 opportunity discovery

Stage 4 should generate and rank **testable opportunity hypotheses**, not product designs. Each hypothesis must pass these gates before it is treated as credible:

1. Name one recurring campaign handoff and the role that loses time, control, or clearance evidence there.
2. Name the incumbent stack (for example, Runway + Frame.io + Sprout) and why a process change, connector, or existing plan does not solve the incident.
3. Distinguish whether the needed record is creative context, version/decision state, publishing authorization, or rights/consent/claim evidence. Do not merge them under “brand safety.”
4. Require recent project artifacts and a measurable consequence: approval delay, rework, duplicate generation cost, missed slot, paid coordination, rejected deliverable, or clearance failure.
5. Treat a finding that agencies are satisfied with current tools, need only better operating discipline, or use AI only for pitch/previz as valid disconfirmation—not as a cue to broaden the concept.

The most useful Stage 4 comparison is therefore **incumbent workflow versus a narrowly defined unresolved handoff**, segmented by campaign risk and cadence. That keeps opportunity discovery within the evidence: no broad “all-in-one agency platform” conclusion is warranted.
